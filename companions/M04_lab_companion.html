Module 4 Learning Goals

In this lab you turn text into numbers that a computer can use. The numbers we turn text into are called a representation. By the end of the lab you should be able to:

  • Build count and TF-IDF tables that you can read row by row.
  • Compare single words with words plus two-word phrases.
  • Protect later tests: learn the word list and weights from development data only.
  • Justify a feature setting for a specific business task.

The main idea: check the word list before you trust any later result. For any number in a table, you should be able to find the word and the message behind it.

Part 1B: Word Counts (Steps 1.6–1.10)

1.6 Count features

CountVectorizer learns one column per word and counts each word in each message. A token is one unit the tool counts; here it is a word of two or more letters, in lower case. The list of columns is the vocabulary. Learning it from data is called fit.

# Lowercase text and keep stop words; the default token rule still applies.
counts = CountVectorizer(lowercase=True, stop_words=None)

# Learn the vocabulary (fit) and count each word in each message (transform).
X_count = counts.fit_transform(dev["light_text"])

# (rows, columns) = (messages, words in the vocabulary).
X_count.shape

Result

(13, 69): 13 messages and 69 word columns.

Companion-added instructor note

Why we keep common words

stop_words=None keeps common words. A stopword is a common word that tools often drop, such as the or no. Here no and not change the meaning of a service message, so we keep them.

1.10 How sparse is the matrix?

A sparse matrix stores only the cells that are not zero. nnz is the number of those cells.

# Share of zero cells = 1 - nonzero cells / all cells.
all_cells = X_count.shape[0] * X_count.shape[1]
zero_share = 1 - X_count.nnz / all_cells
print("nonzero entries:", X_count.nnz)
print("zero share:", round(zero_share, 3))

Part 1C: TF-IDF Weights and Phrases (Steps 1.11–1.16)

1.11 TF-IDF weights

TF-IDF gives each word a weight. TF is how often the word appears in one message. IDF lowers the weight of words that appear in many messages. Each row is then scaled to length 1.

Companion-added instructor note

What a TF-IDF value is not

A TF-IDF value is a coordinate. It is not a probability. It is not an urgency score or a measure of business importance.

1.17 New text uses the frozen vocabulary

After fitting, transform applies the same columns to new text. It does not learn new words.

# A new message: transform only, no refitting.
probe_text = ["Hyperflux charger failed."]
X_probe = unigram.transform(probe_text)

# vocabulary_ is a dictionary from each known word to its column number.
print("hyperflux in vocabulary:", "hyperflux" in unigram.vocabulary_)
print("probe matrix shape:", X_probe.shape)

The made-up word hyperflux is ignored, and no new column appears. Only charger from the probe is a known column. This is why you fit on development data once and then only transform.

2.5 Fit TF-IDF on the training reviews

This is the same idea as step 1.14, with one change for real data: min_df=5 drops terms that appear in fewer than five reviews. Common words such as not stay (stop_words=None), so phrases such as not flattering keep their meaning.

These are the Lab 4 Practice settings: words and two-word phrases, min_df=5, no stop words. Lab 5 uses them again.

# Words and two-word phrases; min_df=5 keeps terms that appear in at least 5 reviews.
practice_tfidf = TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=5, stop_words=None)

# fit learns the vocabulary from train only; transform builds the matrix.
X_train = practice_tfidf.fit_transform(train["review_text"])
X_train.shape

Result

(13581, 23828): 13,581 training reviews and 23,828 words and phrases. This cell takes about a second.

2.6 Which terms set one department apart?

First you average each column over all training reviews. Then you average it over one department's reviews. The difference shows the words that this department's reviews use more than usual.

# The term that belongs to each column of X_train.
practice_features = practice_tfidf.get_feature_names_out()

department_name = "Dresses"
# True/False for each training review. to_numpy() makes a plain array,
# which can select rows of the sparse matrix.
in_department = train["department"].eq(department_name).to_numpy()
# mean(axis=0) averages each column over the rows: one average weight per term.
# np.asarray(...).ravel() turns the result into a flat list of numbers.
overall_mean = np.asarray(X_train.mean(axis=0)).ravel()

# X_train[in_department] keeps only the rows of this department.
department_mean = np.asarray(X_train[in_department].mean(axis=0)).ravel()

# Positive difference: a higher average TF-IDF weight in this department than in all training reviews.
difference = pd.Series(department_mean - overall_mean, index=practice_features)
difference.sort_values(ascending=False).head(25)

Result

A list of 25 terms with their differences. For Dresses the list starts with dress (about 0.050), this dress, the dress and dress is. Further down are slip, petite, wedding and flattering. Common words such as the, it and was also appear: dress reviews use them a little more than other reviews.

Companion-added instructor note

Why subtract the overall average

If you look only at the department's average, common words such as the and it fill the top of the list. The subtraction removes most words that every department uses. The item's own name still comes first, so read further down for what customers say about it.

The cell after Exercise 3 is given in the notebook. It lists single words in validation reviews that are not columns of the fitted vocabulary:

# The analyzer splits a text into the same words and phrases the vectorizer uses.
analyzer = practice_tfidf.build_analyzer()
# vocabulary_ is a dictionary from each known term to its column number.
vocabulary = practice_tfidf.vocabulary_

def unknown_words(text):
    """Take one review and return a sorted list of its single words that are not in the vocabulary."""
    missing = set()
    for term in analyzer(text):
        # Phrases contain a space; we only look at single words.
        if " " not in term and term not in vocabulary:
            missing.add(term)
    return sorted(missing)

# map() calls unknown_words once per validation review.
unknown = valid["review_text"].map(unknown_words)
# True for each review whose list of unknown words is not empty.
has_unknown = unknown.str.len() > 0
print("validation reviews with at least one unknown word:", has_unknown.sum(), "of", len(valid))
unknown[has_unknown].head(5)

Result

The first line reads validation reviews with at least one unknown word: 2320 of 4528. Then five reviews with their unknown words, such as [estimation, reliant, reveiws, savvy, shoppers] and [launders, whit]. Some are typos (reveiws). Companion-added instructor note

Unknown words are normal

About half of the validation reviews contain at least one word the vocabulary does not have. transform ignores these words; it never adds columns. If you refit on validation text to include them, the columns change and validation is no longer a fair check.

B. Reproduce the IDF numbers from the slides

This uses the three short examples from the video. idf_ holds the learned IDF values. Row scaling changes TF-IDF rows, not these values, so the cell only fits the vectorizer and reads idf_.

lx_text = ["refund requested", "refund completed", "refund requested"]

C. Terms that mark low ratings

The merchandising team's second question: which words mark unhappy customers? chi2 scores how strongly each feature is linked to one of two groups: low-rated reviews (1–2 stars) and high-rated reviews (4–5 stars). 3-star reviews are left out. A high score can point to either group, so read each term.

from sklearn.feature_selection import chi2

# True/False arrays: 1-2 stars, 4-5 stars, and either of the two (| means "or").
low = train["rating"].le(2).to_numpy()
high = train["rating"].ge(4).to_numpy()
both = low | high

# chi2 compares the two groups for each column. It returns scores and p-values;
# we keep the scores and ignore the p-values (_).
chi_scores, _ = chi2(X_train[both], low[both])
top_terms = pd.Series(chi_scores, index=practice_features).sort_values(ascending=False).head(15)
top_terms

Result

The top terms are disappointed, going back, cheap, unflattering and not flattering, followed by very disappointed, looked like, poor and wanted to (as in wanted to love). All of these point to the low-rated group.

# The 15 terms from the chi-square list, plus the single word "flattering".
terms_to_check = list(top_terms.index) + ["flattering"]
for term in terms_to_check:
    # The column number of this term in X_train.
    column = practice_tfidf.vocabulary_[term]
    # True for each review where the term has a weight above zero.
    # .toarray() turns the sparse column into a normal array; .ravel() makes it flat.
    has_term = (X_train[:, column] > 0).toarray().ravel()
    low_share = has_term[low].mean()
    high_share = has_term[high].mean()
    # :.1% prints a share as a percentage with one decimal.
    print(f"{term}: in {low_share:.1%} of low-rated and {high_share:.1%} of high-rated reviews")
